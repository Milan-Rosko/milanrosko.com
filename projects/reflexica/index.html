Overview

Reflexica is a program in the philosophy of logic and mathematics, written from the intuitionistic standpoint of constructive logic and proof-theoretic semantics, with Intuitionistic Epistemic Closure as its point of departure. Its formal material comes from proof theory and computability theory (diagonalization, incompleteness, undecidability, and reflection principles), and it applies the questions they raise to decision theory, metaethics, and machine cognition.

A procedure that yields witnesses within an open-ended practice is often taken to certify a completed domain, and the productivity of a representation is taken as evidence for the object it represents. Reflexica examines this passage from local success to global closure in reflexive cases, where the domain contains the procedure that claims closure over it and each attempt at certification reproduces the obstruction.

In each case, the question is whether the passage is warranted or whether the problem already presupposes the closure its procedure was meant to establish. The same question applies to Reflexica: must every bridge from a verified result to a truth claim either transmit constructive warrant or presuppose the realist semantics at issue? A bridge that does neither, a Queer Proof, would refute the account.

What is Reflexica?

1 Introduction

From an intuitionistic perspective [Brouwer, 1913], the distinction between classical and constructive logic resembles a debate between two attitudes toward the existence of what our concepts purport to describe: vitalism and mechanism.

Classical logic, particularly when viewed through a Platonist lens, permits a certain generosity: a proposition is true or false independently of our judgment; hence, the semantic distinction is presumed complete even in the absence of a means of deciding it. In this respect, classical logic resembles the vitalist's confidence that life constitutes something real and unified, irreducible to the particular operations through which it manifests itself. The unity precedes its explanation. A more Brouwerian standpoint partially reverses this order without committing to its ontological opposite. What classical reasoning accepts as a completed division, intuitionistic reasoning approaches more parsimoniously, through the acts by which either side could be established. This latter resemblance lies in the refusal to endow a concept with independent truth merely because its use is productive.

Whether fire or viruses are alive is an old question, and we do not propose to settle it. The distinction between living and nonliving things becomes less secure when formulated at a sufficiently high level of generality: fire consumes, propagates, and maintains itself through exchanges with its environment; viruses reproduce, but only through a host's capabilities. Neither phenomenon compels us to abandon the distinction; rather, each reveals how much depends on the criteria by which it is drawn. The concept remains useful even as the possibility of identifying a single essence corresponding to its entire extension becomes questionable. What we examine instead is how such a distinction behaves under diagonalization, and what it would take to identify the judgment that draws it with any criterion that states it.

Observation:

Every explicit criterion of life supplies its own counterexample. Take metabolism together with reproduction: the sterile mule and the dormant spore fail it, yet we judge them alive; fire, which consumes, grows, and propagates, nearly meets it, yet we judge it lifeless. Each repair of the criterion supplies the material for the next case, and each case is settled not by the criterion but by our judgment of it.

The argument is diagonal but conditional: it succeeds only while our judgment differs from every explicit criterion. Nor is it yet reflexive, since the judgment drives each step without becoming its object; diagonalizing against the judgment itself would require identifying it with some criterion, which nothing in the construction warrants (Section 3.5).

In light of these examples, we reopen the question of the epistemic status of the completeness that classical semantics permits us to assume. The point is not that fire must somehow be both alive and not alive, or that a mathematical proposition must inhabit a third truth value, but that intuition can function with remarkable success long before we have established what, if anything, guarantees its unrestricted application. A completed classification may afford useful compression, yet that usefulness may be mistaken for evidence that the concept has captured an independently completed domain.

Reflexica concerns limits that cannot be certified without reproducing the conditions under which they arise. If the mind is assumed to be finite and computational, the paradigm case is the Halting Problem viewed from within such a mind. The object of scrutiny is a specific passage from formal success to semantic authority: the inference that, because a framework can consistently describe a completed totality, that totality has acquired independent epistemic warrant.

The analysis begins where classical and intuitionistic reasoning appear to have the least reason to disagree: the elementary arithmetic of prime numbers. Book IX, Proposition 20 of Euclid’s Elements [Euclid, 1956] makes no claim about a completed set of primes. It presents what may be read as a proto-constructive procedure:

“Therefore $G$ is not the same with any one of the numbers $A, B,$ and $C$. And by hypothesis it is prime. Therefore the prime numbers $A, B, C,$ and $G$ have been found which are more than the assigned multitude of $A, B,$ and $C$.”

We recall the Tristram Shandy Paradox [Russell, 1903]:

TRISTRAM SHANDY PARADOX

Tristram Shandy needs a year to record each day of his life. After $t$ days he has recorded $\lfloor t/365\rfloor$ of them, so the unrecorded remainder grows without bound. Yet, living forever, he records day $n$ in year $n$, and no day remains unrecorded.

We suspend judgment on whether a procedural reading accepts only the results reached by the construction. Thus, given any finite collection of primes, the procedure constructs a number that is coprime to each member and can be iterated without end.

By contrast, Platonic reasoning treats existential quantification over a completed domain as cost-free, preserving the theorem while obscuring the difference between a domain-level proposition and a witness produced by a construction. Proof-theoretic semantics makes this difference explicit [Gentzen, 1935]. A classical proof admits the existential claim $\exists x:A\,P(x)$; its constructive interpretation supplies data: an object $a:A$ together with evidence for $P(a)$. Under Propositions-as-Types, an inhabitant of $\Sigma_{x:A} P(x)$ represents this information [Howard, 1980].

This distinction places primality in proximity to a negation: a proof that a number is composite provides a finite, extractable, and a priori witness, not merely a certificate. For Compositeness, the factor pair $(a,b)$ supplies the proof’s computational content [Kleene, 1945]. Hence:

Like Halting, where a finished run is its own certificate, Compositeness is directly witnessable: a rectangular arrangement exhibits the factorization. Primality, by contrast, is not known to expose a witness of the same kind. Its defining feature is the exclusion of every such configuration. This exclusion may itself admit a finite positive proof object, but that object certifies the exclusion by quantifying over a finite search rather than by presenting a constituent whose presence makes the number prime.

An example below shows this asymmetry.

The composite can be “easier” to spot:

$$ \begin{array}{ll} 1+1+1 & \quad\quad 1+1+1\\ 1+1+1 & \quad\quad 1+1+1\\ 1+1+1 & \quad\quad 1+0+0\\[2ex] \exists \, d\,(1<d<n\wedge d\mid n) & \quad\quad ? \end{array} $$

We can define Compositeness generally, and thence, provide a finite factor witness $(a,b)$. Bounded exclusion yields the natural form of Primality:

$$ \operatorname{Comp}(n) \;\iff\; \exists a,b\,(1<a<n \wedge 1<b<n \wedge n=ab), $$ $$ \operatorname{Prime}(n) \;\iff\; n>1 \wedge \forall d\,(1<d<n \rightarrow d\nmid n). $$

The left arrangement exhibits a factorization; the right admits no nontrivial rectangle, because seven has no nontrivial divisor. Trial division establishes Primality by exhaustive bounded elimination, and a Pratt Certificate does so with a succinct, recursively checkable proof object [Pratt, 1975]. Both certify that no divisor exists; neither exhibits a constituent whose presence makes the number prime.

Euclid’s argument requires only this exclusion. The constructed number exceeds a common multiple of the assigned primes by one, so none of them divides it, and a decidable bounded search yields a prime divisor $q$ outside the collection, whether the number itself is prime or composite. The argument is therefore intuitionistically valid. What matters is not the existential form but whether a witness is constructed over an open-ended type or quantified over a completed totality.

INTUITIONISTIC FABLE

Nature: These people are asking me whether certain pairs of numbers that differ by two continue to occur—forever, as they call it—but I don’t really know what they mean.

The contemporary restatement, “there are infinitely many primes,” admits two readings. Procedurally, it abbreviates what Euclid proves: for every finite set $S$ of primes, a prime $q\notin S$ can be constructed. Extensionally, it asserts that the primes form a completed infinite totality. The extensional reading introduces no contradiction and leaves local practice intact; it changes only the semantic status of the object it introduces, and this apparent harmlessness makes it easy to accept. Euclid’s argument warrants the first reading. Whether anything warrants the second depends on what “infinity” is taken to contribute:

Razor:

If “infinity” adds no content beyond indefinite continuation, it is ornamental. If it adds a completed totality, it requires an independent warrant.

2 Characterization

2.1 Reflection Mistaken for Authority

By the early twentieth century, logicians and mathematicians could no longer dismiss some philosophical tensions as quirks [Gödel, 1931] [Heyting, 1931]. A theory can remain locally impeccable while its existential conclusions carry a stronger commitment. This commitment comes from how the theory interprets those conclusions and exceeds what its procedure supports. The classical statement also defines an epistemic position.

Assume:

A systematic tendency to infer semantic compression from semantic productivity might not damage ordinary effectivity provided by mathematics or science.

A productive procedure may repeatedly yield intelligible results. Yet its success does not show that it reveals a completed semantic totality, nor must it assume global authority to remain effective.

The Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov Interpretation (or bhk) makes this distinction precise. The proposition $\bot$ admits no construction, while a construction of $\neg A$ is a procedure that transforms any construction of $A$ into $\bot$ [Troelstra and van Dalen, 1988]; Kolmogorov’s reading of propositions as problems gives the same clause its problem-solving form [Kolmogorov, 1932] [Rodin, 2023]. Reflexica asks what happens when agents reify such an absence, treating an obstruction as a semantic primitive.

Drawing on Intuitionistic Type Theory [Martin-Löf, 1984] and constructive mathematics [Beeson, 1985], Reflexica asks whether their insights support empirical predictions in domains where classical logic makes closure claims. Homotopy Type Theory [Univalent Foundations Program, 2013] may merit particular attention.

2.2 The Zeroth World

“Reflexica” is intentionally antiphrastic. Borrowing from Impagliazzo’s Five Worlds Taxonomy for average-case complexity [Impagliazzo, 1995], it names a missing “zeroth world”: not an independently inhabited domain, but a semantic space generated when a theory reflects on its own limits.

Problem:

When does a theory mistake the success of its local operations for evidence that its domain is complete or that those operations reveal an independently existing structure?

The zeroth world arises through a recurring transition. A practice encounters the absence of a method, stabilizes that absence as a problem, and builds it into its semantic architecture. Complexity theory is both an instance and a diagnostic site. A formulation may presuppose a consistent classical totality even when the mathematical practice it supports remains procedural and open-ended. Reading its existential claims as procedural shorthand then conceals the additional commitment to a completed domain.

Our hypothesis is that the obstruction is structural, not merely a limitation of a particular formalism or theorist. When Reflection Principles extend a theory beyond a local obstruction, their productivity may be mistaken for access to an independent domain. The system then iterates its own resources rather than reaching an external source of closure. Greater rigor can intensify the error by concealing self-extension as discovery.

Hypothesis:

Reflexica is a problem within THE theorist, not A theorist, and not A theory.

Reflexica therefore applies this diagnosis to itself. It introduces its semantic space explicitly, parsimoniously, and only once; it neither treats that construction as necessary across domains nor takes Reflexica itself as an independently given object of quantification. This restraint marks the difference between reflective reconstruction and discovery. Without it, operations internal to a framework may appear to disclose an external domain.

A minimal formal analogue is an attempted fixed point of negation. Whatever semantic completeness an agent attributes to its constructions, ordinary finite syntax blocks the simplest identification: no finite formula can be syntactically identical to its own negation.

SIZE ARGUMENT

Let $|A|$ denote the number of nodes in the finite formula tree of $A$. Since implication contributes an additional constructor, we have

$$ |A \to \bot| = 1 + |A| + |\bot| > |A|. $$

Hence $A$ and $A \to \bot$ have different finite tree sizes and therefore cannot be syntactically identical.

The proposed obstruction has the schematic form: for every proposed semantic source or self-model, there is a reflexive circumstance in which every attempted completion fails. Formalizing this $\forall\exists\forall$ pattern requires explicit domains and a precise failure predicate. Its classification within the Arithmetical Hierarchy remains an obligation of that formalization.

The same structural constraint governs logical systems, reification, and human cognition. No agent can reach a semantic standpoint outside the totality that contains that agent. Therefore, no final external criterion can measure the authority of a semantic source.

We nevertheless need stable semantic orientation, or closure. We use language, memory, culture, science, institutions, biography, and technical representation as semantic sources. Humans can systematically reify these sources instead of only using them. Without a semantic totality, a semantic source functions as reality itself. Different access to a shared truth does not primarily explain disagreement about non-constructive content. Such disagreement must arise because agents adopt different semantics that appear complete from the reflexive perspectives that produced them.

Successful semantic practice develops an internal tendency toward semantic absolutization.

Philosophical proposition:

The theologian elevates revelation.
The economist elevates incentives.
The physicist elevates physical law.

The logician should elevate the pathological case: everything.

Our intentionality has limited access to global operational and semantic structure. Naturally, we overestimate the authority of the sources that organize our local experience, so we might mistake operational success for truth. Reflexica begins from here, not from the claim that the mistake is unavoidable.

2.3 Methodology: Reflexica as a Working Hypothesis

Reflexica is methodologically useful before it is established as a general theory. We provisionally assume that an apparent semantic ground may be the stabilized effect of a locally successful practice. This assumption earns its place only if it exposes otherwise hidden commitments, distinguishes cases that a stronger semantics conflates, or supports predictions that could fail. We call this use of a suspected limit limitative abduction.

INTUITIONISTIC EPISTEMIC CLOSURE

REFLEXICA takes INTUITIONISTIC EPISTEMIC CLOSURE as its point of departure: an epistemic discipline that admits propositions through finite, checkable constructions and preserves their warrant under admissible intuitionistic inference, without presupposing a completed semantic domain or unrestricted reflection into truth. Writing $\mathsf{CW}(P)$ for admissible constructive warrant, its central closure condition is

$$ \frac{\mathsf{CW}(P)\qquad\mathsf{CW}(P\to Q)}{\mathsf{CW}(Q)}, $$

where a witness for the conclusion must be effectively obtainable from the witnesses for the premises. Its consistency condition is

$$ \neg\bigl(\mathsf{CW}(P)\wedge\mathsf{CW}(\neg P)\bigr), $$

assumed, not derived: the discipline cannot certify its own consistency. Its limit is stated as explicitly:

$$ \text{inferential closure}\quad\cancel\Longrightarrow\quad\text{semantic completion}. $$

IEC describes a discipline, not an ontology. It fixes how REFLEXICA admits and transmits epistemic commitments; it does not declare that whatever exceeds constructive warrant is nonexistent, meaningless, or false.

IEC is the familiar closure principle of epistemology, if an agent knows $p$ and knows that $p$ entails $q$, then the agent knows $q$, read intuitionistically. Two things change. First, the premises are warrants rather than states of knowledge, and the entailment is itself a construction: $Q$ is warranted because a witness for it is computed, not because it follows semantically. A semantic consequence does not acquire warrant by being true, and a valid argument does not acquire it by being valid; either acquires it only as a checked derivation. Second, the principle is read from its boundary. Closure states not only what warrant reaches but also what it does not: whatever no admissible inference reaches remains outside, however determinate a classical semantics takes it to be. This is the standpoint of proof-theoretic semantics [Gentzen, 1935] [Dummett, 1975] applied to epistemic commitment: what an assertion commits us to is fixed by the rules that warrant it, not by a model given in advance.

Closure here is closure of a discipline under inference, not closure of a domain. Reflexica accepts the first and examines the second. Without excluded middle but with non-contradiction, IEC admits gaps but not gluts: $\neg\mathsf{CW}(P)\wedge\neg\mathsf{CW}(\neg P)$ is a possible state, $\mathsf{CW}(P)\wedge\mathsf{CW}(\neg P)$ is not. The closure condition is used in Section 4.2, the consistency condition by the modules of the Appendix; the limit is what the following sections test. The assumption fixes where Reflexica stands, not how far its diagnosis extends.

The inquiry begins with the operations actually available. For each problem, we identify the witnesses, decisions, or predictions that those operations produce; the domain over which they remain effective; and the point at which their local success is promoted into a claim of closure. Problem Critique names this reconstruction of a problem’s substrate and commitments.

A problem is provisionally impredicative when its proposed criterion of closure already depends on the totality it is meant to evaluate. The next step is reflexive: place the proposed decider, observer, or semantic authority within the domain of its own claim and test whether the original obstruction returns. Recurrence is evidence for the hypothesis, not proof of it.

Failure to find a warrant does not establish that none exists within the specified procedural class; an impossibility within that class does not establish a reflexive obstruction. An investigation may instead eliminate the suspected obstruction and establish that no reflexive problem arises in the case examined. This must remain a genuine outcome of limitative abduction, or the method would repeat the semantic promotion it investigates.

This method favors explicit witnesses, mechanized proofs where available, parsimonious assumptions, and predicative constructions [Feferman, 1964]. It does not reject classical closure in advance. It asks what the closure contributes, what warrants it, and whether the same work can be performed without treating the projected ground as independently given.

The following cases are therefore not assumed to instantiate one structure. Each must independently exhibit the proposed sequence: a local practice succeeds, encounters an absence, stabilizes that absence, and promotes the resulting construction into semantic authority. A resemblance is not enough.

3 Topics

3.1 Construction Without Determination

A construction can become so familiar that we cease to notice what it contributes to the identity of its object. We repeatedly obtain a stable result, recognize it as the same thing, and begin to regard that identity as belonging to the domain itself. Would it survive a change in the representation that made it available? The doubt can be made exact in a finite setting, where every relevant transformation can be inspected.

Consider a simple structure in which this can be checked:

Let $G=C_2\oplus C_2=\{0,a,b,c\}$. The sum of any two distinct nonzero elements is the third, so every permutation of $a$, $b$, $c$ is an automorphism. A nonzero element exists, yet none is fixed by every automorphism:

$$\exists x\in G\;\; x\neq 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \neg\exists x\in G\setminus\{0\}\;\;\forall\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)\;\;\sigma(x)=x.$$

Given $G$ in coordinates, with $a=(1,0)$, $b=(0,1)$, and $c=(1,1)$, a procedure asked for a nonzero element can return $(1,0)$ every time. Repetition makes the choice familiar: this is the element the procedure always finds. Now change coordinates so that $b=(1,0)$. The same procedure returns $b$. Its output remains correct, but the identity of the selected element depends on the coordinates. Each element remains definite. What disappears is the apparent privilege of one element as the element singled out by $G$ itself.

The contribution of a presentation also appears in a familiar explanation of vision. The eye projects an inverted image onto the retina, so, the explanation goes, the brain must flip it back. “Back” makes the explanation seem complete: the brain restores the world to its proper orientation. But why must vision perform this restoration?

BORROWED FRAME FALLACY

A familiar folk diagram shows an upright tree, an eye, and an inverted retinal image. We see the discrepancy and assign the brain a task: turn the image back to match the world. Drawn in the same frame, the upright tree becomes the standard against which the retinal image counts as wrong.

Inversion is a relation: the image is inverted relative to the tree, in the frame that the diagram supplies to both. Nothing in the visual system holds both relata. Turn a running computer upside down: its interface now appears inverted to us, yet nothing in the machine has changed and nothing needs correcting. Its coordinates were never oriented relative to the room; only an observer in the room compares them. The folk explanation borrows that observer’s frame and charges the brain with a correction that exists only within it.

$$ \text{“the image is inverted”} \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad \text{“the brain must turn it back.”} $$

The fallacy credits a relation that holds within the frame of a depiction to the depicted system as an intrinsic property, and derives a task from it. The tree is still seen upright; what disappears is the task of turning it.

The mathematical case exposes a choice supplied by a representation; the visual case exposes a requirement supplied by an explanatory picture. Each invites a question about what we attribute to the object and what its presentation contributes. This gives a doubt about reification an initial, limited form: how much of an object's apparent independence comes from keeping its conditions of presentation fixed?

Outside mathematics, we can rarely specify all the changes of description against which such independence would have to be tested. A moral verdict, a predictor's success, or a machine's fluency may likewise appear to disclose something independently settled in its domain. The following sections examine what warrants that appearance, and what happens when the procedure that sustains it belongs to the domain it describes.

The next section asks how far this doubt can go. The passage from a limit on our procedures to a verdict about their objects now becomes the issue.

3.2 Internal Limit, External Fact

Mackie’s Error Theory [Mackie, 1977] and Rice’s Theorem [Rice, 1953] are both negative about semantics, but in very different ways. Mackie’s denial is ontological: the objective properties that moral discourse presupposes do not exist. Rice’s is procedural and, on its face, more generous: nontrivial extensional properties of programs are well-defined, and only a total computable procedure for deciding them is ruled out. Its verdict is quieter than Mackie’s, but hardly milder. A property granted existence yet withdrawn from every uniform decision survives in extension and is lost to general judgment; for an agent confined to procedures, it is available only case by case. To pass from this internal limit to Mackie’s external conclusion would nonetheless enact the very promotion that Reflexica diagnoses.

ARGUMENT FROM QUEERNESS

Error Theory combines cognitivism with an error thesis: ordinary affirmative moral judgments purport to state objective moral facts, but the objective values they presuppose might not exist. Such judgments could be therefore systematically false.

Objective moral values, if they existed, would be metaphysically unlike anything else in the universe and would require a correspondingly unusual faculty by which they could be known. Their metaphysical and epistemological Queerness therefore counts against their existence.

Mackie links the alleged object to the faculty required to know it. Moral realism posits objective values, while moral judgment appears to require access to them. The Argument from Queerness challenges both demands. For machines, however, property and access must be separated. Nontrivial semantic properties of programs can be well-defined extensionally even when no algorithm can decide them uniformly from program descriptions.

RICE’S THEOREM

Let $\mathcal{PC}$ denote the class of partial computable functions. For every nontrivial extensional property $S$,

$$ \varnothing \subsetneq S \subsetneq \mathcal{PC}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\{e : \varphi_e \in S\}\text{ is undecidable}. $$

The theorem does not make membership in $S$ meaningless, nor does it make every instance unknowable. Restricted classes may be decidable, individual cases may admit proofs, and some properties may be semidecidable. It also does not establish Mackie’s metaphysical conclusion. It identifies a narrower machine-level analogue of the queer faculty: a total, uniformly correct procedure for detecting a nontrivial semantic property of arbitrary programs.

THE REFLEXICA MOMENT

When a domain can represent a proposed total semantic authority, the procedures that claim authority become part of what they must account for. Where the relevant self-application is available, the attempt to certify closure can generate a countercase from the very procedure intended to establish it. The proposed resolution then reproduces the limit it was meant to overcome.

An absent decider is not an absent object, even where both are absent. The REFLEXICA MOMENT occurs when a limit within a practice is taken for a fact about its domain, whether or not that fact obtains.

The investigation of this passage belongs to the same inquiry. What warrants treating its occurrences as manifestations of a common obstruction? REFLEXICA encounters its own question in the attempt to establish the scope of its diagnosis. Whether that attempt yields a warranted generalization, a narrower result, or a further obstruction remains to be determined.

Now assume that a mind is computational. If one of its semantic tasks decides a nontrivial extensional property of arbitrary partial computable functions—or a problem to which the corresponding index set reduces—Rice’s limit applies to the mind’s procedure. Where the domain supports the required self-application, a proposed universal faculty becomes available to the construction that defeats its claim to totality. In this precise sense, the proposition becomes actively diagonal: its demand for closure generates the countercase that prevents closure.

The mind may nevertheless make intelligible and correct local judgments. Reflexica begins when the mind redescribes the limit of its own procedure as an external semantic barrier, or treats its locally successful outputs as facts drawn from a completed semantic totality. The error lies not in every judgment, but in the global authority attributed to the practice that produces them.

Mackie’s case retains a different asymmetry. If an objective moral property were produced, it would refute rather than confirm his Error Theory. Yet the property’s nonappearance does not construct its nonexistence: producing the property would suffice to refute the theory, but its absence does not establish the theory. Formally, by bhk, a construction of $\neg A$ must transform any construction of $A$ into $\bot$ [Troelstra and van Dalen, 1988]. Mackie’s absent object and Rice’s absent decider therefore meet at the level of unavailable warrant, not shared ontology.

The Halting Predicate provides a special computational case with a similar evidential asymmetry [Turing, 1937]. A completed run supplies a finite witness that a computation halts, no unfinished stage of the same run establishes that it never halts, since it may halt later. Non-halting therefore requires evidence beyond the run itself. This one-sided witness structure illustrates the pattern, but it should not be attributed to every property.

HALTING

Given a Turing Machine $M$ and an input $x$, $M$ halts on $x$ if its computation reaches a halting configuration after finitely many transition steps. We write $M(x)\downarrow$.

Rice’s Theorem is therefore not Mackie’s Error Theory. Their failed correspondence is Reflexica. Rice exposes the mechanism by which total internal semantic authority fails; Mackie shows the form assumed when such a failure is projected outward as a fact about reality. Under computational assumptions, a mind encounters Mackie’s form whenever it mistakes the limits of its semantic procedures for the limits of the semantic domain. The following sections test this passage under more exact conditions.

3.3 Newcomb’s Problem as a Case

We now examine a more structured case: Newcomb’s Problem [Nozick, 1969].

Predictive scope is the central issue. The familiar story gives the predictor knowledge of the deliberation that produces the choice. The predictor classifies the outcome of each thought and fills the opaque box accordingly. This assumption is coherent for one agent in one fixed experiment. Reflexive pressure appears when the claim covers every admissible decision procedure. Some procedures include the predictor’s verdict in their deliberation.

NEWCOMB’S PROBLEM

Let there be two boxes. The transparent box contains $1000\,\mathrm{USD}$; the opaque box contains either $B=10^6\,\mathrm{USD}$ or $B=0$. Before the agent chooses, a predictor $P$ predicts whether the agent will take only the opaque box, $C_1$, or both boxes, $C_2$.

It places $10^6\,\mathrm{USD}$ in the opaque box after predicting $C_1$, and nothing in it after predicting $C_2$. The agent then chooses without observing $B$:

$$ \begin{array}{c|cc} & B=10^6 & B=0 \\ \hline C_1 & 10^6 & 0 \\ C_2 & 10^6+1000 & 1000 \end{array} $$

For either fixed value of $B$, $C_2$ pays exactly $1000\,\mathrm{USD}$ more than $C_1$. Let $p=\Pr(B=10^6\,\mathrm{USD}\mid C_1)$ and $q=\Pr(B=10^6\,\mathrm{USD}\mid C_2)$. Conditioning instead on the act gives $\operatorname{EU}(C_1)=10^6p$ and $\operatorname{EU}(C_2)=1000+10^6q$.

Causal Decision Theory (CDT) [Lewis, 1981] holds $B$ fixed under intervention and recommends $C_2$. Evidential Decision Theory (EDT) [Jeffrey, 1965] conditions on the act and recommends $C_1$ when $p-q>10^{-3}$. Both calculations assume the joint relation among the prediction, the box content, and the act. Problem Critique asks which decision procedures this relation can coherently cover.

The crucial quantifier is whatever the agent thinks. Suppose that $P$ is a total, effectively presented predictor. Its verdict is available to each procedure that it evaluates. Let $P(\ulcorner D\urcorner)\in\{C_1,C_2\}$ denote the verdict that $P$ assigns to $D$. Assume that the admissible class supports the relevant predictor-sensitive fixed-point construction. The class then contains a procedure $D_P^{\perp}$ that supplies its own description to $P$ and opposes $P$’s verdict:

$$ D_P^{\perp}=\begin{cases} C_2 & \text{if }P(\ulcorner D_P^{\perp}\urcorner)=C_1,\\ C_1 & \text{if }P(\ulcorner D_P^{\perp}\urcorner)=C_2. \end{cases} $$

If $P$ predicts $C_1$, this procedure chooses $C_2$. If $P$ predicts $C_2$, it chooses $C_1$. Thus, $P$ fails on $D_P^{\perp}$ for either verdict. The universal scope of the prediction claim causes this obstruction. A predictor can remain correct on a restricted class that excludes its verdict from the procedures it evaluates. It cannot remain correct on an open-ended class that supports this predictor-relative construction.

Newcomb’s Problem thus models the wider promotion that Reflexica tracks. The ordinary experiment assumes success for one agent under one information structure. Oraclehood appears when this local success becomes knowledge of every deliberation, including deliberation about the predictor. The prediction then becomes part of the process that it predicts. The evaluator must then close its domain under its own evaluation.

A so-called “stochastic predictor” avoids contradiction because it does not claim total correctness. Its reliability and the resulting payoffs remain well-defined within a fixed model. The diagonal case sets the boundary. Local predictive success cannot cross this boundary and become a total evaluator for predictor-sensitive agents.

3.4 Diagonalization and Diagnosis

Diagonalization can give the preceding case an exact form. An evaluator receives a description whose behavior depends on that evaluation. Incompleteness provides the canonical formal case [Gödel, 1931]. For Reflexica, this case makes the passage from a formal result to its semantic interpretation available for precise examination.

A broadly constructive or antirealist reading ties each mathematical assertion to a construction or another admissible warrant [Heyting, 1931] [Kleene, 1945] [Dummett, 1975]. By contrast, a realist reading treats arithmetic truth as determinate independently of proof [Dummett, 2001] [Frege, 1918]. Both readings accept the formal construction. They disagree about what permits them to read its sentence as true of a completed domain.

For a sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatized theory $\mathrm{T}$, the Diagonal Lemma yields a sentence $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ [Boolos et al., 2007]. The required formal core is compact:

CARNAP'S DIAGONAL LEMMA AND GÖDEL'S DIAGONAL ARGUMENT

$$ \begin{array}{lll} \mathrm{T}&\vdash&\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}} \leftrightarrow \neg \operatorname{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner \mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}\urcorner),\\[1ex] \operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{T})&\Longrightarrow&\mathrm{T} \nvdash \mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}. \end{array} $$

The first line gives an internal fixed-point equivalence. The second gives a metatheoretic consequence for the standard Gödel construction. Neither line alone states that $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is true in $\mathbb{N}$. Both readings accept this formal core but disagree about its semantic authority. An independently warranted consistency claim, and more strongly a soundness claim, lets the realist treat $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ as true but unprovable in $\mathrm{T}$, because the fixed-point equivalence already holds in a true base theory. The antirealist need not treat the result as a truth beyond every possible warrant.

Let $Q$ denote a warrant that decides between the realist and antirealist readings without belonging to either. $Q$ cannot restate the formal result because both readings accept it. If $Q$ has constructive authority, it cannot establish a truth that exceeds possible warrant. If $Q$ has realist authority, it assumes the semantic excess that it must establish. This argument does not prove that no neutral $Q$ can exist. It states what such a warrant must escape.

METALOGICAL PROBLEM

The formal result is shared
$\Downarrow$
Realist and antirealist readings diverge
$\Downarrow$
$Q$ must decide without presupposing either semantics
$\Downarrow$
Every available warrant is interpreted within the dispute
$\Downarrow$
A neutral or natural $Q$ would constitute a Queer Proof

This is a specific contribution of Reflexica. In the Gödel case, $\mathrm{T}$ proves the fixed-point equivalence. The metatheory proves a conditional non-derivability result, and stronger theories can supply more warrants. Promotion occurs when a neutral standpoint treats this extensible sequence as the completed semantic authority of arithmetic truth. A Reflection Principle can extend provability, but its productivity does not certify one correct interpretation of proof [Feferman, 1962]. The diagnostic concerns this promotion, not the limit theorem alone.

The promotion is hard to detect because reflection has a second face, already marked in Section 3.2 as part of the Reflexica moment.

REFLECTION AND ABSENT IMPLICATION

Reflection and the absence of semantic implication behave strikingly alike. A REFLECTION PRINCIPLE asserts the passage from operation to semantic fact: what is derivable is true. By LÖB’S THEOREM, however, a theory derives this passage for $A$ only if it already derives $A$:

$\mathrm{T}\vdash\operatorname{Prov}_{\mathrm{T}}(\ulcorner A\urcorner)\to A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathrm{T}\vdash A.$

Internally, reflection supplies a semantic implication only where none is needed. Where it would add content, the theory behaves exactly as if no such implication existed. Adopted from outside, reflection yields a stronger theory that meets the same absence one stage higher. From within any stage, a reflection that reaches the semantic domain and a mere absence of semantic implication have not been told apart.

We can point at this resemblance, which Löb’s Theorem makes exact [Löb, 1955], but cannot resolve it. A witness that separated the two would be a passage from operation to truth that neither repeats the operation nor presupposes the semantics, which is the neutral $Q$ above, the Queer Proof, in another form.

This diagnosis does not automatically apply to other cases. Each extension must show that a locally successful procedure must certify a total domain that includes its standards of success. Each extension must also show that no witness permits the resulting closure. A resemblance to diagonalization is not sufficient.

The limit applies to both $\mathrm{T}$ and attempts to judge proof from a position outside proof. Consider a Turing Zombie that sharpens this problem. It can prove a diagonal limit, but applying that limit to its own operation requires a warranted connection between the active procedure and the represented system.

3.5 The Turing Zombie

An object-language derivation can formally verify a represented machine description. However, this verification does not show that the represented machine actively performs the verification. Semantic or metatheoretic rules establish this link. A check of the representation cannot recover the link because the representation already assumes it.

The Program must transfer strict diagonalization to less formal settings. Similar patterns appear in computation, linguistic competence, proof theory, and self-modeling. However, this similarity does not show that these domains contain one phenomenon. The narrower question is about self-description. When does self-description require a local procedure to close over the totality to which it belongs?

Consider the following Turing Machine:

$$ \mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,s_0,s_{\mathrm{acc}},s_{\mathrm{rej}}), $$ $$ \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})=\{w\in\Sigma^*: \mathcal{M}\text{ accepts }w\}. $$

At the metalevel, a Tarskian account assigns a semantic extension to the machine’s formal specification [Gruber, 2016]. A specific computation can show that a string belongs to $\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})$. The computation cannot use the language itself as another term. The metalevel defines the language as the extension of the machine’s activity as a whole.

Everyday requests for self-description raise a related question. When Bob asks Alice how many words she knows, she can give only an estimate. Alice must use the same competence to produce the requested inventory. A language model gives a local response to the same question, not a stored census. The request turns an exercised competence into an object to be inventoried: what would warrant treating a response produced by that competence as an exhaustive account of it?

We examine a popular interpretation of incompleteness [Putnam, 1995]:

LUCAS–PENROSE ARGUMENT AGAINST MECHANISM

Let $\mathrm{T}$ be a formal system proposed as a model of mathematical reasoning. By a diagonal argument, there is a sentence $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$. The next step is that the human mathematician can nevertheless see that $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is true. If $\mathrm{T}$ captured the mathematician’s reasoning, then the mathematician would have access to a truth unavailable to the system alleged to model her. Hence, mind exceeds mechanism.

Putnam argues that this act of seeing requires a prior warrant. To assert that $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is true, a person must already have a warrant for a semantic claim about $\mathrm{T}$. More precisely, the person must assume that $\mathrm{T}$ is consistent, or more strongly sound for the relevant arithmetical domain. $\mathrm{T}$ itself does not provide this warrant. The warrant is semantic, which is exactly what “Alice and Bob” cannot determine objectively. Thus, the argument assumes at the moment of insight what it later claims to reveal.

Putnam’s objection addresses the argument’s validity, but it explains only part of the argument’s phenomenological force. The subject does not usually experience herself as assuming consistency before she infers that $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is true. Instead, she experiences herself as directly seeing that $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ is true. Putnam identifies the missing premise but does not explain why this experience feels immediate.

Ordinal analysis reconstructs this immediacy as an open-ended proof-theoretic progression [Feferman, 1962]. When a theory reaches its limit, reflection permits theorists to adopt a stronger standpoint. This standpoint makes additional truths available. The progression supplies increasingly strong warrants while it preserves internal discipline.

Provability logic describes principles for one provability predicate. Polymodal provability logics such as $\mathrm{GLP}$ arrange provability across indexed modalities. In Beklemishev’s analysis, these systems organize iterated Reflection Principles and proof-theoretic ordinal progressions [Beklemishev, 2004]. Turing’s Ordinal Logics use an earlier version of this strategy [Turing, 1939]. When a formal system reaches a consistency or completeness limit, this strategy creates a transfinite sequence of stronger systems. For Reflexica, this tower of explicit standpoints replaces the internal closure of the original system.

We must ask whether this progression is identical with the truth that the subject experiences herself as seeing. No neutral standpoint certifies this identity. Anyone who treats the progression as the semantic content of $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ assumes the authority that the progression must justify. This assumption promotes an extensible sequence of reflected standpoints to a completed semantic object. The progression can produce stronger warrants for $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$. However, no warrant identifies it with the truth that the subject experiences herself as seeing.

The $\mathcal{M}$-analogue is a first-person Halting Problem. A system can produce a verdict about a procedure that the system presents as its own. Likewise, a subject can report that she sees $\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{T}}$ as true for a system $\mathrm{T}$ that represents her reasoning. The problem is not only whether a machine can generate an inscription without a genuine judgment. Assume a consistent calculus, a judgment token, and an operational semantics that validates the token as specified. Does this behavior witness the identity between the active judgment procedure and the procedure whose total correctness the verdict certifies?

This distinction excludes a tempting shortcut. Suppose the metatheory assigns the label $e$ to an executing configuration and adds this reflective rule:

$$\langle e,\sigma,\mathsf{self}\rangle\longrightarrow\ulcorner e\urcorner.$$

A token that asserts $\mathsf{self}=\ulcorner e\urcorner$ can have a valid type. However, the metatheory assigns $e$ as the label of the active procedure. The reduction rule only returns this supplied identity and cannot establish it from within the judgment. A Computational Quine or fixed-point construction can reproduce an index or provide an externally proved extensional fixed point. Neither construction internally witnesses that the current evaluator is the indexed procedure, so metatheoretic indexing assumes the required binding. A Turing Zombie can therefore prove by diagonalization that the Halting Problem is unsolvable for a lesser machine:

TURING ZOMBIE

A Turing Zombie is a proposed computational entity with an asymmetric grasp of diagonal limits. It can use diagonalization to prove that no total computable procedure decides the Halting Problem. It can apply this limit to procedures that it treats as external objects, but lacks a warranted connection between its active procedure and the represented system needed for the intended self-application. Establishing this connection could support restricted self-knowledge. Certifying its complete epistemic reach, including the act of certification, would be a stronger demand.

Our interpretation differs from Carlson’s account of Reinhardt’s Strong Mechanistic Thesis [Carlson, 2000]. We conjecture that $\mathrm{T}\vdash\mathrm{SMT}$ does not imply $\mathrm{T}\vdash K\,\operatorname{TM}(\mathsf{self})$, once the latter claim is precisely defined. SMT binds the knower’s extension, the set of what it knows, to some machine index; it does not bind the active procedure of $\mathrm{T}$ to that index. Proving the non-implication requires definitions of $K\,\operatorname{TM}(\mathsf{self})$ and of the active procedure, and a proof of non-derivability under them; this remains a proof obligation, which we reserve for future work.

Untyped languages often show this distinction directly. Here, identification means operational self-application, not the fact that a system attaches a predicate to code. It also does not mean that an external source assigns the intended referent to a distinguished constant.

DIAGONAL SELF-APPLICATION

Let $\Omega$ be a non-normalizing term, let $I$ be a normal form, and suppose $H$ is a total evaluator that claims to decide whether any quoted term normalizes. In metanotation, form

$$ D=\lambda x. \begin{cases} \Omega, & \text{if }H(\ulcorner x\,x\urcorner)=1,\\ I, & \text{if }H(\ulcorner x\,x\urcorner)=0. \end{cases} $$

Applying the judgment-forming term to itself gives $D\,D$. If $H(\ulcorner D\,D\urcorner)=1$, then $D\,D$ reduces to $\Omega$ and does not normalize. If $H(\ulcorner D\,D\urcorner)=0$, then it reduces to $I$ and does normalize.

The sentence “I am a Turing Machine” adds an operational binding claim to the self-assertion. It does not claim total correctness. A reproduced index or a primitive $\mathsf{self}$ rule from the metatheory cannot establish this binding. Such a rule only returns the binding that the metatheory supplied. If an external source supplies the binding, the system can have restricted correct self-knowledge, but from a vantage point, a Turing Zombie can apply to other machines exactly what it cannot apply to itself.

A large language model makes this construction concrete. A Turing Machine can implement the model and equip it to form, check, and defend a judgment token [Brown et al., 2020]. The token can state whether a mechanistic system recognizes its limits, and it can use a first-person label. The problem is not fluent output, because even a consistent token establishes only what its calculus and operational interpretation permit. An external machine label assumes the first-person binding, but no witness connects this case to the diagonal construction. The construction shows that total correctness over a diagonally closed domain is inconsistent.

When asked, a language model does not identify itself with a specific realization. Its answer is offered as an illustration of the structure, not as evidence for it:

“I have externally supplied reasons to believe that I am implemented on physical hardware, but I do not have direct epistemic access to that fact. [...] The same observable conversation could arise from physical hardware executing a model, from a simulation, or from an abstract Turing-machine computation specified and unfolded within a sufficiently detailed constructive theorem.”

—ChatGPT

One reduces the Lucas–Penrose Argument to a survivorship bias: it counts only the cases in which insight succeeds, all of which concern an external system, and treats them as evidence of a self-application that is never exercised. A system can correctly analyze an external formal object from a stronger standpoint, as the Turing Zombie proves a diagonal limit for an external candidate decider, but applying this result to its own operation requires a warranted connection to that represented system. If $\mathrm{T}$ performs the reasoning, it needs more than the construction. For the standard Gödel sentence, the truth inference uses consistency together with the fixed-point construction and the interpretation of the proof predicate. $\mathrm{T}$ can formulate or adopt that assumption, but under the usual hypotheses of the second incompleteness theorem, a consistent $\mathrm{T}$ cannot prove $\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{T})$.

Ironically, the claim that no confident report of Gödelian self-insight ever shows genuine self-application would make another totalizing inference. The remaining question is narrow: Consider a system that reports Gödelian self-insight with stable confidence. Has any witnessed procedure distinguished genuine self-application from the external diagonal proof available to a Turing Zombie? At present, no procedure has done so. Such a procedure would be the Queer Proof.

Busy Beaver provides a precise example of totalization as an attempted escape [Radó, 1962]. For any fixed number of states and machine convention, there is a determinate maximum number $S(n)$ of steps taken by an $n$-state machine that eventually halts. If that bound were available, it would decide the behavior of every machine in the finite class. Yet the existence of $S(n)$ supplies neither its value nor a certificate of that value, and no uniform procedure computes it. Totalization therefore converts unavailable semantic closure into a completed object and invites us to mistake the object’s determinacy for an available warrant. The obstruction has not disappeared.

3.6 Compression

Consider the following allegory:

THE SPIDER IN THE TUBE

A spider weaves a web across the open end of a hollow pipe. Each strand attaches to the rim or to the existing web, gradually producing a denser and more stable structure.

First, each additional strand improves local coverage, although its marginal contribution may diminish as the web becomes denser.

Second, the attachment points are not uniquely determined. Different configurations can provide comparable support, and each new strand depends on the structure already established.

Third, every strand crossing an existing opening divides it into smaller openings. The web becomes increasingly intricate without ever forming a continuous surface.

The spider can only hope for local progress without global closure. The web can grow indefinitely, but webbing will never provide a uniform surface.

Arguably, the oldest mathematical progress shows this pattern. Incommensurable magnitudes, exhaustion of the circle, the continuum, and the solution of equations repeatedly exceeded the available operations. Each time, an extensible approximation was fixed as a limit, and the limit became an object within a stronger theory: incommensurable magnitudes, for instance, became reals.

The intellectual and scientific culture of the mid-2020s can be characterized as expanding realist and rationalist local control without corresponding global closure. The web is denser, its strands are stronger, and researchers map its local regions with unprecedented precision. At the same time, the number of openings has increased. When a problem includes its own standards of closure, each successful closure becomes another object within the problem.

When the web supports almost every available operation, it becomes difficult to distinguish the web from a surface:

$$ \text{recursive operation} \longrightarrow \text{semantic productivity} \longrightarrow \text{appearance of semantic reality}. $$

Classical mathematics performs the middle transition on a large scale. It compresses operationally different conditions into stable extensional objects and propositions. For an unresolved proposition $P$, Excluded Middle supplies $P\lor\neg P$ before a procedure determines the applicable branch. The proposition closes the partition semantically, but invoking Excluded Middle does not itself supply an operational selector.

$$ \begin{array}{rcl} \text{operational form} &:& \text{produce the applicable branch},\\[1ex] \text{compressed form} &:& \text{reason over the completed partition}. \end{array} $$

Completed domains are the largest form of this compression. They permit quantification over objects without a procedure that generates, inspects, or exhausts the domain. A shorter and more uniform statement can then govern operations that require different data. Compression makes large-scale mathematics possible because it stabilizes the space for mathematical inference.

Consider Beeson's informal version of Church’s Thesis [Beeson, 1985]:

BEESON’S SIMPLIFIED CHURCH’S THESIS

Every rule is reducible to a recursive rule.

The thesis applies to rules that mathematicians and machines can use effectively, but it is painfully close to saying that a completed object is fictional to the extent that its completed existence exceeds the construction that makes it operationally available:

$$ \begin{array}{c} \text{act as though }D\text{ is complete}\\ \cancel\Downarrow\\ D\text{ has been witnessed as a completed semantic totality}. \end{array} $$

The more work a compression supports, the less it appears to be a compression. Its omitted selectors, bounds, and constructions no longer appear as missing information. The stable form then appears to be the reality that inquiry has reached. A productive semantic construction can therefore appear unconstructed. The web resembles a surface when it becomes dense enough to support almost every operation.

Wigner’s problem starts here. Mathematicians develop structures for reasons internal to mathematics. These structures repeatedly support accurate physical descriptions and predictions [Wigner, 1960]. This success does not prove that the resulting physical theory is uniquely appropriate. Effectiveness creates empirical pressure to treat the compressed structure as the reality that it organizes.

The Quine–Putnam Indispensability Argument dogmatically assumes this semantic promotion [Quine, 1980]:

QUINE–PUTNAM INDISPENSABILITY ARGUMENT

Accept the ontology required by the best scientific theories; mathematical entities are so required; therefore accept mathematical entities.

The premise establishes Platonic ideals within a successful representation. The conclusion treats this role as evidence for the ontology that the representation describes. Productivity then becomes authority.

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{recurrent predictive success}&\longrightarrow\text{increased operational warrant},\\ \text{recurrent predictive success}&\cancel\longrightarrow\text{witnessed semantic completion}. \end{aligned} $$

3.7 The Talking Machine

The trained machine intensifies this separation. An effective process implements a language model. During training, finite operations change finitely represented parameters. During inference, finite operations transform a finite input into a finite output [Brown et al., 2020]. Scale, stochasticity, and interaction complicate the process but do not place it outside recursive realization.

The machine still produces context-sensitive language, mathematical arguments, explanations, ontological assertions, and judgments about truth and meaning. It also produces first-person reports and claims about its computational nature. It can argue that mind exceeds computation, although a computational process produces the argument. The key fact is not that the machine calculates. It responds. A recursively realized device learns from the accumulated compressions of human semantic practice and becomes semantically productive.

The stochastic escape treats the operation’s name as another source. It claims that the machine has no semantic structure and only samples from a learned distribution. As an effective description, this claim identifies part of the mechanism. As a semantic explanation, the claim treats the distribution as an extensional totality. This totality covers a projected space of possible continuations, including outputs never produced. It also covers negative conditions that no constructive procedure has settled.

$$ \begin{array}{c} \text{How does a recursive machine produce semantic discourse?}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{It samples from a total probability structure over discourse.} \end{array} $$

The stochastic problem now lies in the measure. At each actual step, the machine computes or approximates weights for represented alternatives. It normalizes the weights, selects a continuation, and repeats the operation. This process has the following finite form:

$$ \text{finite state}\longrightarrow\text{represented weights}\longrightarrow\text{finite selection}\longrightarrow\text{finite output}. $$

The finite operation supplies a witness for the selected token. No finite operation supplies a witness for a completed probability structure over all admissible continuations. Effective selection explains the next operation. The totalized distribution restates the semantic space that gives the operation its interpretation.

Kolmogorov’s account restates the stochastic residue without a stochastic source. It defines descriptions by recursive means [Kolmogorov, 1965]. For a fixed prefix-free universal machine $U$, define the complexity of a finite string $x$ as follows:

$$ K_U(x)=\min\bigl\{|p|:U(p)=x\bigr\}. $$

An algorithmically random string is incompressible. No substantially shorter program produces it under the selected description method. This account makes randomness a relation among an object, a program, and a reference machine. It describes products, not processes: it neither assumes nor excludes an independent stochastic source, and only removes the need to infer one. The Invariance Theorem controls a change of reference machine only up to an additive machine-dependent constant. Exact Kolmogorov Complexity is not computable, so the constructive reformulation exposes its boundary instead of removing it.

$$ \begin{array}{c} \text{randomness as semantic source}\\\Downarrow\\\text{absence of a shorter effective description}\\\Downarrow\\\text{a property relative to the descriptive procedure}. \end{array} $$

Kolmogorov did not provide a foundation for stochastic semantics. Measure returns in Martin-Löf’s definition of random sequences, but as an effective test, not as a semantic source [Martin-Löf, 1966]. What remains is a constructive residue without semantic authority.

The residue preserves the $\Sigma_n^0$/$\Pi_n^0$ asymmetry. Define $H$ and $\overline H$ as follows:

$$ H=\bigl\{\langle M,x\rangle:M(x)\downarrow\bigr\},\quad\overline H=\bigl\{\langle M,x\rangle:M(x)\uparrow\bigr\}. $$

The halting set $H$ is recursively enumerable. A terminating computation supplies a finite positive witness. Its complement $\overline H$ is not recursively enumerable. In general, no finite continuation of a computation shows that the computation never halts [Turing, 1937]. A totalized stochastic representation can still assign probability mass to both halting and non-halting behaviors. This assignment neither enumerates the negative extension nor constructs the truth of membership in that extension.

$$ \underbrace{\left. \begin{array}{c} \text{positive cases with finite witnesses}\\ \text{negative cases without finite witnesses} \end{array} \right\}}_{\\[0.1em]\text{operational asymmetry}} \quad\longmapsto\quad \underbrace{ \text{total stochastic representation} }_{\\[0.1em]\text{extensional compression}}. $$

The expression

$$\Pr(\overline H\mid\text{model})=p$$

does not make $p$ effectively available. It neither decides $\overline H$ nor converts a sample into a non-halting witness. Chaitin’s halting probability $\Omega_U$ is the exact case: it compresses the operational asymmetry into one well-defined real, approximable only from below and itself algorithmically random [Chaitin, 1975]. The compression does not remove the asymmetry from the actual operation.

Solomonoff Induction gives the strongest constructive form of this stochastic claim [Solomonoff, 1964]. Let $U$ be a universal monotone machine. Define $M_U(x)$ as follows:

SOLOMONOFF’S UNIVERSAL SEMIMEASURE

$$ P_x=\bigl\{p:x\preceq U(p)\,\land\,\forall q\prec p\;\,x\npreceq U(q)\bigr\},\quad M_U(x)=\sum_{p\in P_x}2^{-|p|}. $$

Here $q\prec p$ means that $q$ is a proper prefix of $p$. Minimality concerns producing $x$, not halting, and keeps extensions of a program from being counted again. The universal semimeasure is lower semicomputable but not computable. Programs reveal positive contributions when they generate the prefix. In general, no procedure can show that no other program will contribute. Unresolved nontermination prevents closure of the remainder. A totalized representation includes this remainder without deciding it and keeps it distinct from observed terminating contributions.

STOCHASTIC FORK

If stochasticity is effective, it is another recursively realized operation. If stochasticity is total, its totality remains unwitnessed.

Effective stochasticity computes approximate weights and produces finite selections. Ideal stochasticity uses exact shortest descriptions, an exact universal distribution, or a measure over all programs. It can also use a completed division between random and non-random objects. Effective stochasticity explains local production without semantic authority. Ideal stochasticity restores the completed semantic object that the algorithmic account aimed to replace. Whether these alternatives exhaust the possible sources of semantic authority remains to be established.

“Stochastic parrot” also objects to missing grounding and intent [Bender et al., 2021]; only its stochastic half concerns us here. It dismisses the operation but does not change its evidential force. The following operations can produce all observable signs that support semantic attribution:

$$ \text{recursive transformations}+\text{compressed human inscriptions}+\text{effective selection}. $$

The output can contain semantics without a semantic source outside those operations. The label accepts semantic productivity but assumes the distinction that it must explain. The same question applies to human judgment, proof, and classical discourse. What additional witness shows that their semantic productivity exceeds recursively realized compression? The word “stochastic” supplies no witness.

The machine’s discourse does not witness a standpoint over the semantic totalities that it invokes. Instead, the discourse shows that recursive compression can produce the appearance and practical effects of such a standpoint. The machine uses finite inscriptions and effective transitions. Its output still presents stable objects, meanings, and perspectives as if their semantic field were already complete.

A recursively realized machine can show semantic productivity without a witness of total semantic authority.

$$ \operatorname{Recursive}(\mathcal{M})\wedge\operatorname{SemanticProductivity}(\mathcal{M})\wedge\neg\operatorname{Witnessed}_{\mathcal{E}}\bigl(\operatorname{TotalAuthority}(\mathcal{M})\bigr). $$

The final conjunct is relative to the evidence $\mathcal{E}$ presented here: no item of $\mathcal{E}$ witnesses total authority. The machine can manipulate judgment tokens coherently within a consistent calculus. The question is what warrants binding the active evaluator to the procedure represented in the total-authority claim. The language model makes this question concrete by sustaining semantic productivity while the warrant for semantic totality remains to be supplied.

This result returns the argument to the Turing Zombie. A computational system can correctly state and defend a diagonal limit. Applying that limit to its own operation requires a warranted connection between the active procedure and the represented system. Reflexica asks what establishes this connection and how far the resulting self-assessment extends.

The phenomenon forces a choice at the level of warrant. Either a witness shows that the machine has the semantic authority that its discourse appears to express, or semantic productivity does not license that authority. On the reading examined here, the first branch would take the form of a witness that the recursively realized system has become an oracle over its semantic domain; connecting semantic authority to oraclehood requires its own definition and argument. This domain contains the system’s interpretations and verdicts. The machine’s discourse cannot provide that witness because it assumes the inference under examination.

The first branch is the Queer Proof. It requires a witnessed passage from recursive operation to authority over the total semantic domain. This domain provides the interpretation of the operation. Training performance supplies no such passage. At the level of warrant, training performance supports the second branch: oracle-like semantic behavior does not establish oraclehood.

The machine displays semantic productivity, but the evidence presented here does not establish its authority over the semantic totalities its discourse invokes. This leaves a question for any account that treats such productivity as evidence of that authority: what warrants the inference? Reflexica locates the disputed passage here.

The argument yields an abductive and empirical result:

$$ \begin{array}{c} \text{classical compression}\\\Downarrow\\\text{effective realization}\\\Downarrow\\\text{stochasticity invoked as the residual source}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{Kolmogorov returns randomness to effective description}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{exact randomness and universal probability retain}\\ \text{the nonconstructive remainder}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{the realized compression seems to “talk back”}. \end{array} $$

No independent semantic source enters the sequence. The successful operations produce the appearance of such a source. This effect explains why semantic promotion is difficult to resist. The following restricts this promotion:

Razor:

A semantic interpretation gains no authority by being compressed into a classical proposition, assigned a probability, or redescribed as randomness. Where its effect is recursively realized, infer the realized operation. Any further semantic authority requires a witness not already contained in the totalization.

The principle is intuitionistic because it binds the claim’s licensed semantic content to the construction that witnesses it. It is a razor because it cuts the surplus passage from a productive representation rule to a completed structure. The principle does not refute the structure. It denies that productivity supplies a witness for the structure.

3.8 Recurrence Is Not Identity

The razor applies also to Reflexica. The same diagnosis recurs across mathematics, computation, ethics, and self-modeling. This recurrence gives the compression operational and explanatory force. However, this success does not establish Reflexica as the completed structure of every case. With $I_{\mathrm{loc}}$ the cases examined and $I$ the projected class of all cases:

$$ \operatorname{ExplanatoryProductivity} \bigl(\mathrm{Reflexica},I_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigr) \quad\cancel\Rightarrow\quad \operatorname{OneStructure} \bigl(\mathrm{Reflexica},I\bigr). $$

Reflexica is a semantic compression whose usefulness can produce its own reality effect. Cross-domain identity requires more than recurrence, shared vocabulary, or explanatory success. It also requires conditions under which the reflective structure remains unchanged when the substrate changes. The Program must determine those conditions.

4 The Program

Reflexica erects no ultrafinitist restriction on formal mathematics; its aim remains the search for the assumptions by which formal or procedural success acquires semantic authority. Before this diagnosis supports a general limitative conclusion, the Program must specify its proposed structure and satisfy its central proof obligation.

4.1 The Reflexica Schema

The proposed structure is local cognition $\longrightarrow$ projected totality $\longrightarrow$ reflexive instability. More explicitly, $P(x)$ is locally performable $\rightsquigarrow$ $T(P)$ is globally assertible $\rightsquigarrow$ $P$ becomes entangled with its own totalization.

REFLEXICA SCHEMA

Let $D$ be a projected domain with locally witnessed subdomain $D_{\mathrm{loc}} \subseteq D$, let $\mathcal{W}$ be a witness space with adequacy relation $W(x,w)$, let $\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{D}$ be a class of admissible witness-assignments, and let $\Delta \colon \mathcal{F} \to D$ be a constructor. Write $x_F := \Delta(F)$ for the case generated relative to a proposed assignment $F\in\mathcal{F}$.

$$ \underbrace{ \forall x \in D_{\mathrm{loc}}\; \exists w \in \mathcal{W}\; W(x,w)}_{\\[0.1em]\mathrm{local\ witnessability}} \quad \rightsquigarrow \quad \underbrace{\exists F \in \mathcal{F}\; \forall x \in D\; W\!\left(x,F(x)\right)}_{\\[0.1em]\mathrm{projected\ totality}} $$

Suppose, additionally, that the constructor defeats every proposed total witness-assignment at the case it generates:

$$ \underbrace{\forall F \in \mathcal{F}\;\neg W\!\left(\Delta(F),F\!\left(\Delta(F)\right)\right)}_{\\[0.1em]\mathrm{reflexive\ instability}} $$

Then no admissible assignment totalizes:

$$ \neg\exists F \in \mathcal{F}\; \forall x \in D\; W\!\left(x,F(x)\right). $$

If local witnessability is established separately, local success and global failure hold together, and the projection fails.

The schema does not infer failure from the absence of a known witness. Each application must specify its domain, witness relation, and class of assignments, then establish the failure condition for its constructor. The cases examined here motivate this structure; their resemblance does not prove that they instantiate it identically. In computational applications the constructor acts on descriptions rather than extensions, and the indexed form $\forall e\in E\;\neg W(\Delta(e),F_e(\Delta(e)))$ is more faithful. The projection $\rightsquigarrow$ also combines three obligations: selecting local witnesses, staying within $\mathcal{F}$, and extending from $D_{\mathrm{loc}}$ to $D$. Instability need not attack the last one. In the halting case, if witnesses mean correct Boolean verdicts, one may classically take $D_{\mathrm{loc}}=D$, yet no total computable assignment supplies all of them. This does not assert constructive access to a verdict for every input.

The schema should be distinguished from Lawvere’s Fixed-Point Theorem [Lawvere, 1969]. There, reflexivity is a hypothesis:

LAWVERE'S FIXED-POINT THEOREM

Given a point-surjective map $A \to B^A$, every endomap of $B$ has a fixed point.

The Reflexica Schema assumes only a case-generating constructor and an admissible class of assignments. Each application must establish the stated defeat property; any relationship to Lawvere’s representation hypothesis requires a separate argument. The Schema then asks whether a system can witness its own reflexivity, or whether reflexivity is always supplied by the metatheory that interprets the system.

The mathematical novelty, if any, must lie in discovering nontrivial preservation invariants and proving obstruction theorems for specified reflection operators, not in the general schema alone.

The metatheory can itself be formalized, which suggests closing the regress by a circle. One can imagine a circle of implementations and interpretations linking sequent calculus, Turing machines, $\lambda$-calculus, and dependent type theory, with Lawvere’s Theorem formalized within the circle:

$$ S_1 \xrightarrow{E_1} S_2 \xrightarrow{E_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{E_{n-1}} S_n \xrightarrow{E_n} S_1. $$

The arrows need not express the same relation: execution, derivability, normalization, and interpretation must be distinguished. A machine executing a proof checker for a type theory does not thereby establish that theory’s soundness. Let $E_i\colon S_i\to S_{i+1}$ be the translations, with $S_{n+1}=S_1$. The circle is faithful for the relevant arithmetic sentences when every arrow preserves and reflects derivability:

$$ S_i\vdash A\iff S_{i+1}\vdash E_i(A). $$

The composite $E_n\circ\cdots\circ E_1\colon S_1\to S_1$ preserves and reflects derivability for the specified sentences. This closes the circle at the level of those derivations. The foundational question concerns the status of the circle itself: when each framework can be represented within another, what warrants treating any member—or their mutual adequacy—as a ground? A soundness argument introduces an interpretation and its assumptions; formalizing that argument within another member makes those commitments part of the circle’s own objects of investigation.

Internal consistency claims give this question a precise form. Suppose the translations are effective on proofs and $S_1$ proves their correctness, so that $S_1\vdash\operatorname{Prov}_{S_1}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner)\to\operatorname{Prov}_{S_k}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner)$, hence $S_1\vdash\operatorname{Con}(S_k)\to\operatorname{Con}(S_1)$. Under the usual hypotheses of Gödel’s Second Incompleteness Theorem, a consistent $S_1$ then proves the consistency of no member $S_k$. The particular circle above remains a proposed construction until its systems and translations are specified.

CLOSED CIRCLE

REFLEXICA concerns the passage from a closed circle of mutually adequate formal representations to a claim of semantic authority. Each system may implement, interpret, or verify another. Closing the circle establishes the specified formal relationships; it does not by itself warrant the semantic interpretation attributed to the circle as a whole. The circle is a formal construction; that its closure supplies semantic authority is the disputed additional inference.

4.2 The Internal Problem

What independently justified bridge, if any, permits a locally warranted operation to claim authority over its own realization or a completed semantic domain?

QUEER PROOF

A QUEER PROOF is a passage from a verified result $S$ to the truth of $A$ that neither transmits constructive warrant to $A$ nor presupposes the realist semantics at issue. It would establish $\mathbb{N}\models A\wedge\neg\mathsf{CW}(A)$, separating an unprovable truth from no truth at all, without being a proof of either in the sense IEC admits. A single QUEER PROOF would refute REFLEXICA.

A constructive bridge transmits warrant by application. A purely semantic bridge appears to assume the disputed account of truth. The conceptual partition might exclude the falsifier by definition. After a bridge succeeds, the partition could classify it as another warrant or an assumption of realism.

THE DECISIVE QUESTION

Is the warrant/semantic-presupposition dilemma derived, or stipulated?

The constructive meaning of implication gives one branch. Let $S_x$ state that a proposed problem $x$ has a verified solution. Let $A$ be the target assertion. As in Section 2.3, $\mathsf{CW}(P)$ means that $P$ has constructive warrant in the relevant open-ended sense. The closure condition of IEC gives closure under application:

$$ \mathsf{CW}(S_x)\;\land\;\mathsf{CW}(S_x\to A) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathsf{CW}(A). $$

This result shows that a constructively warranted bridge cannot transmit truth without also transmitting warrant. It does not show that every other truth-transmitting bridge assumes realism, so a separate exhaustiveness claim is necessary. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be an independently specified class of admissible bridges. Let $\mathsf{Transmits}(B,S_x,A)$ mean that $B$ licenses passage from the verified solution to $A$. Let $\mathsf{CWB}(B)$ mark constructive warrant, and let $\mathsf{RP}(B,A)$ mark authority that assumes the realist semantics. The missing result has this form:

$$ \forall B\in\mathcal{B}\, \Bigl( \mathsf{Transmits}(B,S_x,A) \to \bigl(\mathsf{CWB}(B)\lor\mathsf{RP}(B,A)\bigr) \Bigr). $$

The Program must classify the obligations of semantic promotion. It must not exclude the third possibility by definition. Specifically, it must:

  • specify the admissible bridge space $\mathcal{B}$ independently of the dilemma;
  • state what transmission preserves: truth, validity, assertibility, evidence, or some other norm;
  • identify the constructor or application rule by which a verified solution is connected to the target assertion;
  • give a non-circular criterion for when a bridge presupposes realist semantics; and
  • specify an independently characterized counterinstance: a bridge that transmits truth while satisfying neither branch.

If the Program derives the exhaustiveness claim from sufficiently weak and explicit assumptions, the claim gives Reflexica a strong core. If the claim depends on a prior verificationist account of assertion, Reflexica remains a development of that account. In that case, Reflexica can draw its limitative conclusions only under that account. The Appendix states the detailed falsifiability problem.

A second obligation concerns transfer across domains: under what conditions is the reflective structure preserved when its substrate changes? An advance claim of identity would turn bounded correspondences into an unwitnessed total unity. The Program must instead determine when the structure is inherited and whether its diagnosis produces results beyond redescribing familiar limits.

5 Adequacy and Falsification

A question can be valid within a formal system without being an adequate formulation of what an inquiry seeks to settle. Validity fixes the consequences of stipulated rules. Adequacy requires a further argument: that the distinctions drawn by those rules capture the relevant possibilities, and that their formal closure deserves the semantic authority assigned to it. Reflexica concerns this second passage.

This changes the burden of proof. Reflexica need not establish that a valid question is meaningless, permanently insoluble, or based on a nonexistent object. Any such verdict would claim the external standpoint that the Program rejects. Instead, a proposed application must identify the local procedure, the totality projected from it, the act of semantic promotion, and the reflexive mechanism that defeats closure. The stronger interpretation remains responsible for showing why the formal division is adequate and why local success warrants authority over the completed domain.

Assume that a consistent human is a Turing machine. The assumption appears to dissolve the distinction between human and mechanical cognition by identifying both with recursive operation. Yet the identification changes nothing operationally. The human continues to produce judgments, descriptions, and questions about the nature of the machine.

The apparent resolution thus reconstructs the original problem. What counts as a machine? What establishes that the active procedure is identical with the formal object used to describe it? The computational assumption permits an answer within a selected formal interpretation, but does not independently certify the interpretation's authority. This is the question the Turing Zombie of Section 3.5 isolates; here it serves as a test of adequacy rather than as a further case.

SEMANTIC DISSOCIATION

A sufficient structural condition for dissociation obtains when an identifying operation is itself included within the domain of its identification, while the identification preserves the relevant operations without independently establishing the semantic authority of their description. The more completely the identification incorporates the operations that evaluate its adequacy, the less its completeness can serve as independent evidence of that adequacy.

The result is not a contradiction between the propositions that the human is and is not a machine. It is a failure of the inference from operational preservation to semantic exhaustion. What appeared to eliminate the distinction has instead displaced it into the relation between the operation and its description. The dissociation therefore entails neither computationalism nor its denial; it restricts the justificatory transitions on which both positions rely, not their possible truth.

Mere difficulty is therefore not sufficient for Reflexica. A genuine case must show that the demand for closure depends on a totality that includes, revises, or otherwise entangles the means by which closure is judged. This criterion separates a reflexive obstruction from an ordinary unsolved problem without pretending to decide the case in advance.

We assume, by abduction rather than deduction, that a third-person system could prove the missing theorem.

Prediction:

As recursively implemented systems improve, they should extend mathematical competence on problems with fixed statements and independently checkable solutions. In cases designated as reflexive in advance, further competence should instead yield stronger local theorems, conditional results, barriers, and reformulations while exposing the dependence of the original criterion of closure.

The Program as a whole faces the stronger falsifier developed above: an independently specified Queer Proof that transmits truth without transmitting constructive warrant and without assuming the realist semantics at issue.

These criteria preserve the distinction between a productive diagnosis and a verdict imposed in advance. Formal validity does not settle adequacy, and the failure of one route to closure does not establish that closure is impossible.

6 Philosophy and Purpose

Why do demands for globally sufficient justification repeatedly arise from locally intelligible constructive practices? What happens when those demands are applied to the procedures intended to satisfy them?

Mathematical questions of absence, totality, and closure motivate this inquiry. Seeking a common explanation brings the inquiry itself into view: what would warrant treating heterogeneous cases as manifestations of one structure? Reflexica studies this demand alongside those that prompted it. Its own attempt to explain the pattern becomes another case to investigate.

The question is therefore both what warrants a proposed closure and what warrants the criteria by which that closure is assessed. Identifying this further demand opens an investigation; establishing whether it produces an obstruction remains work to be done.

The preceding argument updates an intuitionist complaint: realism adds to classical logic a commitment that the logic itself does not entail. In Dummett’s account, an unresolved case is a gap in our knowledge of a determinate fact [Dummett, 2001] [Dummett, 1975]. Reflexica asks what warrants this commitment when the procedures of inquiry leave the case open.

This question brings apparently distant cases into relation: a reflective proof system, a moral theory unable to instantiate its diagnosed error, and a predictor ranging over agents whose choices depend on its verdict. In each case, the task is to identify the passage from a practice’s local success to a claim of completed closure, and to examine the justification offered for it. A common vocabulary makes that passage available for comparison while keeping its particular commitments visible.

Heidegger’s claim that the sciences cannot use the Nichts as their object offers one interpretation of this boundary [Heidegger, 1929]. On the constructive interpretation proposed here, a system encounters the boundary when its demand for closure exceeds the witnesses it can produce. Where an effective description supports self-application, diagonalization may turn that demand against a proposed total evaluator. This is a condition to establish in each case, not a universal consequence of description as such.

Reflexica therefore speaks from within the constructive standpoint it employs. It does not claim that every possible description must exhibit the same limit, nor does it infer the nonexistence of semantic objects from the failure of a procedure intended to decide them; either inference would repeat the promotion under examination. In this sense, the most rigorous nihilism nihilizes nihilism itself: withholding warrant from semantic content, it must also withhold warrant from the denial of that content. Instead, Reflexica looks inward and finds the culprit of commonality there. This is why it is inherently intuitionistic.

7 Conclusion

Reflexica investigates a recurring difficulty in the relation between effective procedures and the semantic domains they are taken to describe. A procedure may produce verifiable results without establishing the completeness of the domain over which those results are interpreted. The difference matters when a claim of closure includes the procedures, observers, or criteria by which closure is judged.

The cases examined here—Rice’s Theorem, Newcomb’s Problem, diagonal constructions, and the Turing Zombie—show where the passage from local competence to global semantic authority requires independent justification. The Reflexica schema states this passage as local witnessability, projected totality, and reflexive instability. Each application must establish its obstruction under the assumptions of the case, not infer it from a missing witness.

A general limitative conclusion remains conditional. The division between bridges that transmit constructive warrant and bridges that presuppose realist semantics has not been shown to be exhaustive, and the Queer Proof names the counterinstance that would defeat it. Until the division is proved or defeated, the schema remains a proposal whose applications must be assessed separately.

The idea leads toward a single principle of warrant. In the notation of Section 4.2:

$$\neg\mathsf{CW}(S)\;\not\Longrightarrow\;\mathsf{CW}(\neg S).$$

The absence of warrant is not a warrant of absence, and $\neg\mathsf{CW}(S)\land\neg\mathsf{CW}(\neg S)$ remains a possible state: a disciplined suspension, not a gap over a determinate fact.

The diagnosis is not meant to be the end point. Reflexica seeks to remain productive after sublimation: once a projected totality is withdrawn as semantic authority and retained as a working compression, the limit it marked should yield stronger local theorems, conditional results, barriers, and reformulations, as the prediction of Section 5 expects. Reflexica therefore neither replaces classical reasoning with a universal constructive criterion nor infers the nonexistence of semantic objects from the failure of procedures intended to decide them. It keeps distinct what a practice witnesses, what its interpretation projects, and what further inquiry must establish.

An instrument may productively investigate even its own limitations without thereby acquiring the authority to declare the ultimate semantic nature of what it has found. A mind in the middle of a stroke may still diagnose it. The diagnosis is an achievement; what was diagnosed remains another question.

APPENDIX

The Problem of Metalogical Falsifiability

The Problem of Metalogical Falsifiability concerns the possibility of a result that distinguishes truth from possible constructive warrant without presupposing the semantic distinction it is intended to establish.

Consider an open-ended, warrant-based antirealist position according to which an assertion of mathematical truth requires an admissible construction or other constructive warrant. A proposed refutation must establish more than the independence of a proposition from a particular formal system. It must establish that a proposition is true although no possible constructive warrant can establish it.

Let $\mathsf{CW}(P)$ denote the existence of constructive warrant for a proposition $P$, understood in this open-ended sense rather than as provability within a fixed calculus. The intended counterexample would satisfy

$$ \mathbb{N}\models A\ \wedge\ \neg\mathsf{CW}(A). $$

The difficulty concerns how such a conjunction could be established without already assuming that arithmetic truth is independent of possible warrant.

Suppose a constructively verified result $S$ is proposed as evidence for $A$. A bridge from $S$ to $A$ must license the conclusion without either supplying constructive warrant for $A$ or presupposing the realist semantics under dispute. If the bridge is constructively warranted, closure under constructive implication gives

$$ \mathsf{CW}(S),\quad \mathsf{CW}(S\to A) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathsf{CW}(A). $$

The result therefore cannot establish the absence of possible constructive warrant for $A$.

Alternatively, the bridge may assert a semantic consequence,

$$ S\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}\models A, $$

without providing a construction of $A$. Its force then depends upon the interpretation of truth in the standard model. If that interpretation already assumes the independence of truth from constructive warrant, the bridge presupposes what the proposed refutation must establish.

These alternatives motivate the search for a third possibility. Let $\mathsf{NeutralBridge}(S,A)$ schematically denote a relation that licenses the passage from a verified result $S$ to the truth of $A$, without providing constructive warrant for $A$ or relying on the realist semantics at issue. A candidate Queer Proof would then satisfy

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathsf{QP}(S,A):={}& \mathsf{CW}(S)\\ &\wedge\mathsf{NeutralBridge}(S,A)\\ &\wedge\mathbb{N}\models A\\ &\wedge\neg\mathsf{CW}(A). \end{aligned} $$

This expression specifies the intended conditions rather than defining an independently established class of objects. Because constructive warrant is deliberately open-ended, $\mathsf{CW}$ is not yet a predicate of a fixed formal language. Both it and $\mathsf{NeutralBridge}$ require precise interpretations before their conjunction can be evaluated within a formal theory. Likewise, $\mathbb{N}\models A$ states the target claim under examination; it is not introduced here as a neutral definition of truth.

The distinction between relative unprovability and absence of possible warrant is essential. For a fixed formal system $C$, the conjunction

$$ \mathbb{N}\models A\ \land\ C\nvdash A $$

may hold under appropriate metatheoretic assumptions. Such a result distinguishes truth from provability in $C$. It does not establish that $A$ exceeds every possible constructive warrant. A stronger metatheory may itself provide a warrant for the claims that $C$ cannot establish.

The central open problem is therefore whether the distinction between constructive truth transmission and realist semantic presupposition exhausts the admissible bridges from verified results to mathematical truth.

An affirmative answer would require an independently specified domain of admissible bridges and a proof that every such bridge falls under one of these alternatives. A negative answer would require a counterexample whose authority does not depend on either alternative.

A conditional lemma sharpens the first alternative. The intended conjunction has the form of Fitch’s knowability paradox [Fitch, 1963]. Suppose that $\mathsf{CW}$ distributes over conjunction, that it is factive, and that a warrant for $\mathbb{N}\models A$ yields $\mathsf{CW}(A)$. Then $\mathsf{CW}(\mathbb{N}\models A\wedge\neg\mathsf{CW}(A))$ yields both $\mathsf{CW}(A)$ and $\neg\mathsf{CW}(A)$. Under these assumptions, a Queer Proof can never itself be constructively warranted. The truth-to-warrant transfer principle requires separate justification, so the lemma derives the constructive alternative only conditionally. It does not establish that every nonconstructive bridge presupposes realism.

The closure argument above establishes neither result. It identifies the obstruction faced by the familiar forms of truth transmission and specifies what a proposed alternative must overcome. The existence or impossibility of such an alternative remains an open obligation of the Reflexica Program.

Formalization

Proofcase develops the formal point of departure through three connected modules: M001, L001, and L002. They specify what counts as an accepted construction, isolate a conditional obstruction at a negation fixed point, and examine the one-way mirror relation that remains compatible with consistency.

M001: Epistemic Closure

M001 gives a minimal instance of the Intuitionistic Epistemic Closure (IEC) assumed in Section 2.3. Its formulas use implication and falsity, and its finite proof scripts are accepted by a total Boolean checker. The development supplies checked proof transformations for modus ponens, deduction, and constructive negation introduction. Acceptance thus has an explicit witness: a finite script that passes the checker under the specified rules and assumptions.

L001: The Aporetic Lemma

L001 studies an acceptance predicate $C$ closed under modus ponens. A supplied evaluation frame yields a formula $B$ for which $C$ accepts both directions of the equivalence between $B$ and its negation. Here $\neg B$ abbreviates $B\to\bot$. Under this fixed-point condition, accepting either side yields accepted falsity:

$$ C(B\to\neg B)\;\land\;C(\neg B\to B) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigl(C(B)\lor C(\neg B)\bigr)\to C(\bot). $$

If $C$ is consistent, neither side is accepted. The result separates failure to accept $B$ from acceptance of $\neg B$. Its application depends on the stated fixed-point and closure premises. In particular, the equivalence concerns accepted implications between formulas, rather than syntactic identity of a finite formula with its negation.

L002: The Mirror Lemma

L002 works with M001’s checked derivability and a one-way mirror condition. Write $\Gamma\vdash_M A$ for checked derivability under theory $M$ and context $\Gamma$. The mirror condition is $\Gamma\vdash_M\neg A\to A$. Under consistency, it excludes a checked refutation of $A$:

$$ \Gamma\vdash_M\neg A\to A,\quad \Gamma\nvdash_M\bot \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma\nvdash_M\neg A. $$

L002 names this non-refutability condition $\mathsf{AsIF}$. It permits explicit reasoning under the additional assumption $A$, while keeping that assumption distinct from acceptance in the original context. The defined recognition mechanism would supply the missing fixed-point direction, so consistency excludes its acceptance at the mirror position. The Recursive Mirror Lemma carries the obstruction through finite iterations when the mirror step preserves the required adequacy.

These results give the distinction between unavailable warrant and warranted negation a precise setting. Their scope is checked derivability under explicit premises. For an intended application, deriving the mirror condition from an independently justified construction, rather than stipulating it, remains an open obligation. Connecting them to the open-ended $\mathsf{CW}$ of the preceding section requires a further interpretation, and the exhaustiveness of the warrant/semantic-presupposition dilemma remains an obligation of the Program.
